# Best Portable Power Stations for RV Living

You're boondocking off a forest service road in Montana, the furnace fan has been cycling all night, and at 6 a.m. the inverter starts beeping. Your house batteries are at 11.8 volts, the coffee maker is out of the question, and the nearest hookups are two hours away. That's the moment RVers start shopping for a portable power station — not as a camping gadget, but as a second battery bank that can run the CPAP, keep the fridge cold, and bridge the gap until the sun comes back. The trick is that RV life is harder on these units than tent camping is: they live in a vibrating metal box, they get charged from a cigarette-lighter port or a roof panel, and they have to survive a 105°F afternoon in a closed storage bay. This guide covers what actually holds up.

## What to look for

- **Usable capacity vs. nameplate capacity.** A "1,000Wh" station doesn't give you 1,000Wh at the outlet — expect 85–90% after inverter losses. Size for the loads you run overnight, and remember lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries tolerate deep cycling far better than older NMC cells if you're discharging daily.
- **Continuous output, not peak.** Your microwave might spike to 1,500W for a second, but the station needs to *sustain* whatever your biggest load draws. Match continuous watts to your heaviest appliance and leave headroom.
- **Charging paths that fit an RV.** Look for solar input voltage ranges that match real panels (not just the brand's own), a 12V DC input you can wire to your house bank, and AC charging that won't trip a 15A campground pedestal.
- **Pass-through and UPS behavior.** If you want to run your rig's 120V circuits through the station while plugged into shore power, the unit needs clean pass-through and a fast transfer time. Not every model does this well.

## FAQ

**How many watt-hours do I actually need for a night of boondocking?**
Add up what runs overnight: a 12V compressor fridge draws roughly 30–50W average, a CPAP about 30–60W, furnace fan 40–80W, plus phone and laptop charging. Most couples land around 800–1,200Wh per night, which is why the 1,400–2,000Wh class is the practical sweet spot for RV use.

**Can I charge a portable power station from my RV's 12V socket while driving?**
Yes, but the cigarette-lighter port typically delivers only 100–200W, so a 2kWh station takes many hours of driving to refill. For meaningful alternator charging, look for a unit with a higher-wattage DC input or wire a dedicated DC-to-DC charger — check the input specs before buying.

**Will a portable power station run my RV's air conditioner?**
A 13,500 BTU RV air conditioner draws roughly 1,300–1,600W running with a higher startup surge, so you need a station with at least 2,000W continuous and good surge handling — the Renogy Lycan 5000 or the larger EcoFlow and BLUETTI units can do it for a few hours. Smaller 1,500W units generally can't sustain it.

**Is LiFePO4 worth the extra cost over older lithium chemistries for RV use?**
If you're cycling the station most days, yes — LiFePO4 is rated for thousands of cycles versus roughly 500–800 for typical NMC packs, so it lasts far longer under daily discharge. If you only boondock a few nights a month, the lighter weight of NMC may be worth the tradeoff.

**Can I leave a power station in an unheated RV storage bay in summer or winter?**
Most manufacturers specify an operating and charging temperature range, and charging below freezing can damage lithium cells — check the spec sheet for your model. In hot weather, keep the unit out of direct sun and ventilated, since high heat accelerates battery degradation.
