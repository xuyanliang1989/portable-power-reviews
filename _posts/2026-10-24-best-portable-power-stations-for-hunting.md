You're 4 miles back in a draw when the temperature drops into the twenties, your phone is dead, and the GPS on your wrist is flashing low battery. Or maybe it's the wall tent at 5 a.m., the coffee pot dead on the camp stove, and the CPAP you need to sleep through a second night with no generator humming outside. Hunting has quietly become a battery-powered sport — trail cameras, GPS units, heated insoles, headlamps, and comms all need juice — and a portable power station is how you keep it all running when the truck is a mile away and the nearest outlet is a memory. This guide walks through what actually matters for a hunting rig and rounds up the stations worth your money right now.

## What to look for

- **Real usable capacity vs. headline watts.** A station rated 1,000Wh will run your gear for roughly 800–900Wh before you hit the low-battery cutoff. Match capacity to your trip length, not the biggest number on the box.
- **Cold-weather behavior.** Lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) handles cold better than older chemistries, but charging below freezing is still a no-go on most units. Look for a model with low-temp charge protection if you hunt late season.
- **Weight and carry.** A 1,000Wh station often weighs 25–30 lb. If you're packing it in on foot, capacity means nothing if you can't carry it. Wheels and a solid handle matter for basecamp rigs.
- **Outputs and surge.** Trail cameras and phones are easy. A CPAP, electric cooler, or small heater pulls more. Check continuous and surge wattage, plus whether it has a 12V/car port and USB-C PD for fast top-offs.

## FAQ

**Can a portable power station run a CPAP all night?**
Yes — most 1,000Wh stations will run a standard CPAP at a moderate pressure setting for 8–10 hours, often with capacity to spare if you turn off the humidifier. Check your CPAP's wattage (usually 30–60W without humidifier) and size your station accordingly; a 1kWh unit is a safe bet for one night, and a 1,500Wh unit covers two.

**Will these stations work in freezing temperatures?**
Most LiFePO4 stations will discharge in cold weather but won't charge below freezing — the battery management system blocks charging to protect the cells. Keep the station in the tent or truck cab overnight and only charge it once it's warmed up, or look for a model with low-temp charge protection.

**How much solar do I need to recharge at camp?**
As a rule of thumb, a 200W folding panel will recharge a 1kWh station in about 6–8 hours of good sun, and a 400W setup cuts that roughly in half. Real-world output is lower than rated, so plan for 60–70% of the panel's rated wattage in practice.

**Can I run a small electric heater or coffee maker off one of these?**
A 1,500–1,800W station can run a small coffee maker or a low-wattage space heater briefly, but heating appliances drain capacity fast — a 1,000W heater will empty a 1kWh station in about an hour. For sustained heat, a propane or diesel heater is far more efficient.

**Are these stations safe to leave in a vehicle or tent?**
LiFePO4 stations are generally safe and don't produce fumes, but avoid leaving them in a hot vehicle in direct sun, and don't charge them in an enclosed space without ventilation. Keep them dry, and store them at partial charge (around 50–60%) if you won't use them for months.
