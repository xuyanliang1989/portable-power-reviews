The deadline is in forty minutes. Your laptop is at 61%, the Wi-Fi router just blinked off, and the whole neighborhood went dark with a transformer pop you felt through the floor. You grab a flashlight, find your phone hotspot, and realize the real problem: your cable modem needs wall power, your second monitor is dead, and the desk lamp that was holding your sanity together is gone. That's the moment a portable power station stops being a camping gadget and becomes a piece of home-office infrastructure. The good news is you don't need a $5,000 whole-home battery to keep working through an outage — you need the right size, the right outputs, and a realistic idea of how long your gear will actually run.

## What to look for

- **Capacity in watt-hours (Wh), not marketing watts.** Add up your real loads: a laptop charger (~60W), a monitor (~30W), a router + modem (~20W), a desk lamp (~10W). A 500Wh unit runs that ~120W office for roughly three to four hours after conversion losses; a 1,000Wh unit doubles it.
- **Pure sine wave output.** Electronics and anything with a motor or compressor want clean power. Every unit below is pure sine wave — confirm it on the spec sheet, because modified sine wave can whine, overheat, or refuse to charge some devices.
- **UPS-style switching.** For a home office, the fastest switchover (ideally under 20ms) means your desktop and router ride through the outage without rebooting. Not every station offers true pass-through UPS mode; check the manual.
- **Recharge speed and solar input.** A station that refills from the wall in under two hours is far more useful during a multi-day storm than one that takes eight. A 200–400W solar input lets you top up during a long outage.

## FAQ

**How long will a 1,000Wh power station run my home office?**
A typical desk setup — laptop, one or two monitors, router, modem, and a lamp — draws roughly 100–150W. After conversion losses, a 1,000Wh station runs that load for about five to seven hours. Add a space heater or a printer and runtime drops fast, so size up if you have high-draw gear.

**Do I need a pure sine wave inverter for my computer?**
Yes. Pure sine wave output matches the power from your wall outlet, which is what computers, monitors, and network gear expect. Every station in this roundup is pure sine wave; modified sine wave units can cause buzzing, overheating, or refusal to charge on some electronics.

**Can a portable power station act as a UPS for my desktop?**
Some can, but only if the manufacturer specifies a fast switchover (ideally under 20ms) and pass-through UPS mode. The BLUETTI AC180 is the clearest example here. For most laptops, which have their own batteries, a UPS mode isn't necessary — the laptop just keeps running on its internal battery while the station powers the router.

**How much solar do I need to recharge during an outage?**
Match your panel wattage to the station's solar input ceiling. A 200W panel will recharge a 1,000Wh unit in roughly six to eight hours of good sun; a 400–600W array cuts that in half. If you live somewhere cloudy, plan on wall charging between outages rather than relying solely on solar.

**Is it safe to leave a power station plugged in all the time?**
LiFePO4 units are generally fine to leave plugged in, and many have a charge-limit setting (often 80–90%) that extends battery life if you keep them topped up. Check your unit's manual for the recommended storage charge, and avoid leaving any station in a hot garage or direct sun.
